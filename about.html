About

Te Kura Rau Mahara is an interdisciplinary research group that works with biosignals and wearable technology for the empowerment of people. It is a collaboration between Human Centred Computing (HCC), School of Computing and Mathematical Sciences, School of Engineering, Te Kotahi Research Institute, School of Health, and the AI Institute. Te Kura Rau Mahara aims to prioritize and recognise the contribution of Māori knowledge in all academic disciplines; conduct research that prioritizes people through a focus on biosignals; and create a space dedicated to innovation, interdisciplinary research and collaboration.

“Te Kura Rau Mahara” emerged at the University of Waikato when four researchers—each working at different edges of neurotechnology, computer science, prosthetics, health, and Indigenous ways of knowing—saw an opportunity to braid their strengths. When the lab formally opened its doors in early 2025, that collaboration became a powerful force for good. At Te Kura Rau Mahara we treat every biosignal—EEG, EMG, measures of cognitive load—not as raw data to be extracted, but as a taonga that, when carefully interpreted through both mātauranga Māori and Western science, can be transformed into tools for health, rehabilitation, learning, and enhanced human capability. Our work is increasingly being recognised internationally as a beacon for how Indigenous knowledge systems and cutting-edge technology research can stand together, mutually informing design, data governance, and translation into real-world impact. In this lab, technology becomes a bridge: connecting whānau, students, and global research partners in the shared task of revealing what has always been present—human potential in relationship with place, community, and technology.

About the Name

Pronounciation: Te Kura Rau Mahara​

Phonetic: Teh Koo-rah Roh Mah-hah-rah

Breakdown: Te – /tɛ/ (like "ten" with a short "e") Kura – /ˈkʉɾa/ ("kʉ" as in New Zealand English "food"; /ɾ/ is a tapped 'r') Rau – /ˈɾaʉ/ ("raʉ" like "row" in NZ English, with a rolled/tapped 'r') Mahara – /ˈmahaɾa/ ("ma" as in "mama", clear "h", tapped 'r') The tapped /ɾ/ is common in Māori and sounds like a soft 'd' or quick 'r'.

​Meaning of Name:​ ​The use of 'te' suggests that this is 'the' kura signaling primacy. ​‘Kura’ is used in many contexts and can mean “red” “scarlet” “red feather” “to be educated or schooled” or “treasure”​ ​'rau’ was considered for its multiplicity of meanings that include: “to put into”, “gather into”, “place into” “to catch (with a net)”, “plumeage” “picking up a beat”; and finally “hundreds”​ Mahara is “to think about”, “to remember”, “to recollect”, “to know”, “to be thoughtful”, “to reason”, this includes the inception of ideas and intellect. With these considerations it does not make sense to have the title limited to one meaning. This name takes on multiple meanings at different levels of understanding. On a basic level this lab could be considered “the school of thought”, however it is more than that. It is ‘the’ place where the complexities of thought, intellect and reasoning are treasured in a way that is uniquely Māori.

Meaning of Tohu / Imagery

This tohu is more than a symbol—it is a powerful statement of intent. Its imagery is rich, layered, and deeply interdisciplinary, drawing strength from both tradition and innovation. At its heart lies a commitment to uplift and centre Māori knowledge in spaces where it has long been underrepresented. Through patterns of haehae and whakarare, the design honours the collective nature of creation, echoing the narrative of Hineahuone, the maiden formed from clay. Every line, curve, and form within this tohu contributes to a larger, living whole—an embodiment of the enduring, interconnected strength of Māori knowledge systems. The deliberate visual connections between heads and mouths speak to the transmission of knowledge, reflected in the presence of atua such as Roiho and Roake. Just like the multitude of atua that gathered to contribute to Hineahuone, this lab brings together people, ideas, and purpose to contribute meaningfully to world-leading research and technology—infusing it with depth, context, and another valuable perspective. More than design, this tohu is a visual whakapapa. It offers Aotearoa—and the world—a vital perspective on technology and health, one grounded in legacy, enriched by culture, and guided by Māori knowledge.
